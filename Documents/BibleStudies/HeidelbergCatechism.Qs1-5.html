Lord's Day 1 — Our Only Comfort in Life and Death

Q.1. What is your only comfort in life and death?

Ans: That I am not my own,1 but belong with body and soul, both in life and in death,2 to my faithful Saviour Jesus Christ.3 He has fully paid for all my sins with his precious blood,4 and has set me free from all the power of the devil.5 He also preserves me in such a way6 that without the will of my heavenly Father not a hair can fall from my head;7 indeed, all things must work together for my salvation.8 Therefore, by his Holy Spirit he also assures me of eternal life9 and makes me heartily willing and ready from now on to live for him.10

1 1 Cor 6:19, 20.
2 Rom 14:7-9.
3 1 Cor 3:23; Tit 2:14.
4 1 Pet 1:18, 19; 1 Jn 1:7; 2:2.
5 Jn 8:34-36; Heb 2:14, 15; 1 Jn 3:8.
6 Jn 6:39, 40; 10:27-30; 2 Thess 3:3; 1 Pet 1:5.
7 Mt 10:29-31; Lk 21:16-18.
8 Rom 8:28.
9 Rom 8:15, 16; 2 Cor 1:21, 22; 5:5; Eph 1:13, 14.
10 Rom 8:14.

Lord’s Day 1 is a summary of the Heidelberg Catechism and reflects its dual character of devotional and doctrinal content. The key word here is “only” for there are many methods that might appear to give us comfort in life and in death but, according to the catechism, there is really only one comfort and that is the comfort of belonging to the Lord Jesus Christ. To put this another way, when the chips are down, if we think that our friends or our drugs or our false religion can give us comfort then we are sadly deceived. Only the Lord Jesus Christ has the power to deliver us from death which he has promised to do so on the day of his return when he raises our bodies from the grave.

In Exodus 3:6 God identifies himself to Moses by the words “I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob.” It is easy to miss the significance of the tense of the verb “to be” in this statement; after all, Abraham had been dead for some 650 years so we would expect God to have said "I was the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. So the present tense ("am"") proves that for the saints there is life after death; after all, Jesus says, “He is not the God of the dead but of the living” (Mark 12:27). Jesus' idea of life and death is quite different from how we think and talk about them in day to day conversation. He clearly implies here that there is a sense in which the saints (Christians) continue to live even after they have died physically. This life is described as "eternal life" and is defined in John 17:3

And this is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.
This "knowledge" is the relationship we enjoy with Christ both in this life and the next.

In 1 Peter 1:18-19 the apostle Peter describes the transaction by which we became the property of the Lord Jesus Christ.

For you know that it was not with perishable things such as silver or gold that you were redeemed from the empty way of life handed down to you from your ancestors, 19 but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or defect.

This describes the value that Christ puts upon us as his own personal property. And if death were to dispossess us how could the cost be justified by the few short years we spend on earth.

The catechism goes on to say that “he has fully paid for all my sins with his precious blood, and has set me free from all the power of the devil”. So we see that included in the transaction was the judicial payment for “all my sins”. By “all” is meant sins past, present and future. It was enough for Adam and Eve to commit one sin in order to plunge themselves and their entire posterity into hell for all time. And that sin was not murder or adultery or treason but the eating of forbidden fruit! How much worse are the sins of those of us who have had opportunity to commit all manner of evil and yet to have all of those sins, even the ones we haven’t committed yet, paid for in full by the precious blood of the Saviour. If we are not bowled over by the very thought then it is because we have not given it much thought.

Then there is the matter of preservation:

He also preserves me in such a way that without the will of my heavenly Father not a hair can fall from my head; indeed, all things must work together for my salvation.
In case we have any doubt about the care that Christ exercises over us, Matthew 10:20-31 describes the intensiveness of that care. So how do we reconcile that with the tribulations that life serves up to all of us from time to time? The catechism answers that by reminding us of Romans 8:28:
And we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him, who have been called according to his purpose.

We know that the greatest good that God does for us is our salvation since his greatest act of love for us was in the saving work of his Son (John 3:16). And because he is the Lord of all history he is able to work all events together, i.e., not one event by itself but all events taken together, resulting in our highest good, which is our salvation.

Another passage that describes this process is Hebrews 12:4-13 on the matter of discipline. In verse 7 we read, “Endure hardship as discipline; God is treating you as his children.” So we ought not put hardship down to “bad luck”. It is not luck or chance that orders our experience but the sovereign hand of God himself whose purpose it is to make us holy without which we cannot see the Lord (Heb. 12:15).

None of these benefits would have much impact upon us in this life were we not convinced of their reality. To achieve this the catechism tells us:

Therefore, by his Holy Spirit he also assures me of eternal life and makes me heartily willing and ready from now on to live for him.

This means that God does not leave us in the same condition in which he found us prior to conversion, for the Holy Spirit takes up residence within us in order to create such things as saving faith, a love for God and assurance of eternal life. How could we derive comfort from Christ if we had no assurance of these things? So they are supernaturally imparted. This, in turn, inclines our hearts to live for Him which is no flash in the pan but a lifelong commitment.

We can summarise these comforts as follows:
1. We belong to Christ.
2. The debt of our sin is fully paid for.
3. We have freedom from the power of the devil.
4. We are prevented from falling away.
5. We are assured of eternal life.
6. We are made heartily willing and ready to live for him.

Q.2. What do you need to know in order to live and die in the joy of this comfort?

Ans: How great my sins and misery are1; how I am delivered from all my sins and misery2 how I am to be thankful to God for such deliverance3

1Rom 3:9, 10; 1 Jn 1:10.
2Jn 17:3; Acts 4:12; 10:43.
3Mt 5:16; Rom 6:13; Eph 5:8-10; 1 Pet 2:9, 10.

Question 2 of the catechism is about the knowledge we need to have in order to enter the state of salvation. Merely knowing what Christ has done for his people is not enough to put us in possession of this state. In other words we need to know what the gospel is which we must believe in order to (a) have all of our sins paid for; (b) be set free from the power of the devil; (c) be the object of God’s special saving providence; and (d) be heartily willing and ready to live for him.

We will deal with clauses a – c separately.

1. “How great my sins and misery are”. We have already seen that saving faith is supernaturally wrought in the heart at the point of conversion. That faith is a total resting in Christ exclusively for salvation. There can be no hedging of bets when it comes to this faith. To be able to have such faith we must come to despair of all other avenues, particularly ourselves. This results from the conviction of sin that is necessary for true conversion and is peculiar to the work of the Holy Spirit. When we have this conviction we have no trouble believing that without Christ we are eternally lost and condemned sinners, bound for hell with nothing to commend ourselves to God.

It is not hard to see why the unregenerate man cannot bring himself to accept such a diagnosis and it is the main sticking point as why many cannot accept the gospel.

2. “How I am delivered from all my sins and misery”. This is the next thing the unsaved person needs to know and is behind the cry of the Philippian jailer, “What must I do to be saved?” (Acts 16:30).

We have already seen that to be saved we must put our faith wholly and exclusively in Christ for the removal of all of our sins. This faith is a trusting in another to do something we cannot do ourselves and is entirely consistent with the impotence we experience when convicted by the Holy Spirit.

Combined with this faith is a hearty sorrow at having sinned against God. It is not enough to have regrets over the evil we have committed against people. This will come in good time, but the repentance we are talking about is a God directed change of heart, a sorrow at having offended God in our thoughts, our words and our deeds. Again, this is not a sorrow we can manufacture. Like saving faith, it is wrought in the heart by the Holy Spirit along with a firm desire to live for him for the rest of our days.

3. "How I am to be thankful to God for such deliverance". The third thing we need to know is how we can be thankful for the deliverance we have in Christ. We can't just go out and buy God a box of chocolates and a thank you card. God owns all the chocolate in the world, including the world itself. So what does he want from us? In Romans 6:13 we read the following:

Do not offer any part of yourself to sin as an instrument of wickedness, but rather offer yourselves to God as those who have been brought from death to life; and offer every part of yourself to him as an instrument of righteousness.

We learn what righteousness is by reading the bible, particularly the ten commandments and their application. Jesus explains the ten commandments in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5 - 7). Following conversion we must attach ourselves to a bible believing and Christ-centred church which focuses its attention on the Lord Jesus Christ and his teachings.

Lord's Day 2 — Our Sin and Misery

Q.3. From where do you know your sins and misery?
Ans: From the law of God.1
1Romans 3:20; 7:7-25.

In Romans 3:20 we read:
For by works of the law no human being will be justified in his sight, since through the law comes knowledge of sin.

We have already seen in our answer to question 2 that we need to know our sins and our miserable estate in order to enjoy the comfort of question 1. There are two reasons for this:
1. Saving faith is a resting in Christ alone for salvation. This means that we cannot hedge our bets when it comes to trusting in Christ for salvation. The Galatian Judaizers tried that by adding circumcision to the gospel and Paul disqualified their faith as bogus.
2. We need to know how serious our sins are in order to appreciate the deliverance that God gives us from our sin and its judicial consequences. Comparing ourselves to others won't achieve this because the people we compare ourselves to are are every bit as wretched as we are. The biblical standard is embodied in the law which is summarised in the ten commandments. An in depth explanation of their demands is given by Christ in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7) and it is only when we study this explanation that we begin to get an accurate picture of just how degenerate we really are.

Q.4. What does God's law require of us?
Ans: Christ teaches us this in a summary in Matthew 22:34-40.
34 Hearing that Jesus had silenced the Sadducees, the Pharisees got together. 35 One of them, an expert in the law, tested him with this question: 36 “Teacher, which is the greatest commandment in the Law?” 37 Jesus replied: “‘Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind.’ 38 This is the first and greatest commandment. 39 And the second is like it: ‘Love your neighbor as yourself.’ 40 All the Law and the Prophets hang on these two commandments.”

In his answer to the Sadducees Jesus condenses the ten commandments (decalogue) into two. The first is a summary of the first four commandments of the decalogue and concerns the love we ought to have for God. This love is absolutely unique in that it involves the whole heart and soul and mind. It means we must love him with every molecule of our being. We are not to hold anything back from God in respect to our devotion to him. Our love for God should trump every other type of love. This means that our love for spouse, family and friends must always take second place when compared to loving God.

This doesn't mean that a Christian must leave his/her marriage, family or friends in order to love God. But it does mean that if any one of these gets in the way of our devotion to God then God must win every time. A good way to explain this is in the case where an unbelieving husband refuses to allow his wife to read her bible or attend church services. The wife must disobey her husband and leave the consequences to God. Or if a spiritually hostile government outlaws the public assembly of Christians, those Christians must break the law by meeting underground.

The second commandment in Matthew 22:37-38 concerns our love for the neighbour. We notice first that we are not commanded to love him/her with all our heart and mind and soul. Only God get's that level of devotion. Instead we are to love them with the same devotion with which we love ourselves. We note here that self-love is assumed in this discourse. Since the advent of the psychology of self-esteem we might get the impression that not all people love themselves and that self-love is an important index of psychological health. It is suggested, however, that if the Lord Jesus Christ assumes self-love, then so should we.

Q.5. Can you keep all this perfectly?
Ans: No, I am inclined by nature to hate God and my neighbour.

It seems unreasonable that God would command us to do something which we are incapable of performing. The reason the catechism gives for this is that despite the work of regeneration of the Holy Spirit in our hearts we retain a sinful nature that hates God and our neighbour. This sinful nature has its origin in the fall of Adam in the Garden of Eden (Genesis 3:6 ff). The result was a sinful nature that spread to all of his descendants and which can only be overcome by the supernatural work of the Holy Spirit, beginning with regeneration (rebirth). This overcoming of the sinful nature is called "sanctification" and is a gradual process in all Christians ending only in death when the soul is extracted from the body and taken into the presence of the ascended Christ. Meanwhile, the Christian must do battle with this sinful nature that hates God and the neighbour. He must resist temptation, and when he falls he must get up again, confess his sin from the heart, and keep going.
