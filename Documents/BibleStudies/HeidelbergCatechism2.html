Lord's Day 3: Q's 6 - 8

Q. 6. Did God, then, create man so wicked and perverse?
Ans: No, on the contrary, God created man good (1) and in his image (2) that is, in true righteousness and holiness (3) so that he might rightly know God his Creator (4) heartily love him, and live with him in eternal blessedness to praise and glorify him (5).
1. Gen 1:31.
2. Gen 1:26, 27.
3. Eph 4:24.
4. Col 3:10.
5. Ps 8.

Lord's day 3 concerns the origin of sin in man and is a defense against the charge that God created man in his fallen state and is somehow responsible for his imperfections. The catechism clearly presents its case in the light of Genesis 1 & 2 where we are told that God created man in his own image. This does not mean that man was created to look like God but to have certain attributes in common, namely, righteousness, holiness and true knowledge. In this form man was able to fellowship with God, i.e., to know him, love him, and live with him in eternal blessedness.

Q. 7. From where, then, did man’s depraved nature come?

Ans: From the fall and disobedience of our first parents, Adam and Eve, in Paradise (1) for there our nature became so corrupt (2) that we are all conceived and born in sin (3).

1. Gen 3.
2. Rom 5:12, 18, 19.
3. Ps 51:5.

So how does a perfect man fall into sin? This question has vexed theologians and philosophers alike and the closest we can get to a satisfying answer is that God created Adam and Eve in a state whereby they were able to sin. The four moral conditions identified by Reformed theologians are as follows:

1. At creation...............Able to sin.
2. At the fall.................Not able not to sin.
3. At conversion..........Able not to sin.
4. In glory.....................Not able to sin.

The fact that we are conceived and born in sin means that we all inherit a sinful nature from our first parents (not able to not sin). And not only do we inherit a sinful nature but we also inherit the very guilt of Adam who was our federal head and representative in the Garden of Eden. This is the burden of Paul's argument in Romans 5:12-20.

Q.8. But are we so corrupt that we are totally unable to do any good and inclined to all evil?

Ans: Yes (1) unless we are regenerated by the Spirit of God (2).

1. Gen. 6:5; 8:21; Job 14:4; Is 53:6.
2. Jn. 3:3-5.

To understand question eight we need to understand how the catechism, and therefore the scriptures (see proof texts), define "good" and "evil". At the heart of the sinful nature is a hatred of God. This hatred is not a fist shaking rage at the heavens but a life of willful ignorance of God and his divine authority (Romans 1:18-32). There is no love for God in the heart of the unregenerate whatsoever. This means that love for God and a desire to glorify him are never motives for doing anything on the part of the unbeliever. And this is evil in the sight of God. Consequently, the "good" is any act of obedience to the revealed will of God that is born of a genuine love for God, which only the regenerate are capable of. And because the very best works of the Christian are not perfect, they are cleansed by the mediatorial work of Christ who forever intercedes for his people.

Lord's Day 4: Q's 9 - 11

Q.9. But does not God do man an injustice by requiring in his law what man cannot do?

Ans: No, for God so created man that he was able to do it (1). But man, at the instigation of the devil (2), in deliberate disobedience (3) robbed himself and all his descendants of these gifts (4).

1. Gen 1:31.
2. Gen 3:13; Jn 8:44; 1 Tim 2:13, 14.
3. Gen 3:6.
4. Rom 5:12, 18, 19.
Man's inability to keep God's law is not a case of man wanting to keep it but finding it beyond his reach. In other words, he is not like an athlete who fails to win a race despite his best effort. In this case there is a hearty desire to win but a physical inability to do so. But in the case of man's inability to keep the law there is no such desire. He simply does not want to keep it and therefore chooses not to.

To illustrate: putting a meat dish before a horse will never tempt it to eat it. The horse cannot even want to eat the meat because it is not, by nature, a carnivore. Similarly, putting the law of God before an unregenerate sinner will never tempt him to keep it. The unregenerate can never want to keep it because it is his nature to break it. That's not to say that he does not have the ability to perform the works of the law mechanically. Can he love his neighbour? Yes. Can he perform acts of worship? Yes. So don't these things amount to law obedience? No they don't because they lack one vital component: love for God. Unless an act is done out of love for God it is worth nothing to God. If you discovered that your friend gave you a birthday present with strings attached you would not be impressed. As we have already seen in the answer to question 4, Jesus summarises the law in terms of loving God and the neighbour. Our love of God is foundational to loving the neighbour. Without it, love for the neighbour turns into idolatry.

Q. 10. Will God allow such disobedience and apostasy to go unpunished?
Ans: Certainly not. He is terribly displeased with our original sin as well as our actual sins. Therefore he will punish them by a just judgment both now and eternally (1) as he has declared (2). Cursed is everyone who does not continue to do everything written in the Book of the Law (Gal 3:10).

1. Gen 2:17; Ex 34:7; Ps 5:4-6; 7:11; Nahum 1:2; Rom 1:18; 5:12; Eph 5:6; Heb 9:27.
2. Deut 27:26.
A common sentiment expressed among unbelievers and even some Christians is that if God were loving why did he not simply forgive Adam and Eve and start over? After all, it was only forbidden fruit, wasn't it? It is not for us to judge the judge of all the earth (Gen. 18:25) but what seems disproportionate to us can be, and in this case is, a moral failure of catastrophic proportion. We think nothing of what to us seem peccadillos but to God, who is holy beyond all human comprehension, they amount to cosmic treason. For God to have made light of original sin would have compromised his holy and righteous character. We cannot appreciate the seriousness of sin mainly because we sin every day. So we take it on faith that God is just in all his ways despite how those ways may appear to us.

The second thing to notice in the answer to question 10 is the reference to eternal punishment; after all, eternity is an awefully long time for someone to suffer. Further to what has already been said concerning God's justice, people remain in hell eternally because they never repent. We have already seen that the unregenerate sinful nature prevents the sinner from doing anything good; this includes repentance. In other words, the occupants of hell never repent despite the misery of their existence there. That is not to say that they like being in hell; they don't. Jesus tells us that hell is a place of wailing and gnashing of teeth. But given their hatred of God, they would hate heaven even more. We know that heaven is full of the presence of God, whom they continue to hate. To be in hell, then, is to be the ultimate missfit.

Q. 11. But is God not also merciful?

Ans: God is indeed merciful (1) but he is also just (2). His justice requires that sin committed against the most high majesty of God also be punished with the most severe, that is, with everlasting, punishment of body and soul (3).

1. Ex 20:6; 34:6, 7; Ps 103:8, 9.
2. Ex 20:5; 34:7; Deut 7:9-11; Ps 5:4-6; Heb 10:30, 31.
3. Mt 25:45, 46.

Question 11 concerns the reconciliation of two seemingly irreconcilable attributes of God: his justice and his mercy. His justice is about punishment for sin, and his mercy is about forgiveness of sin. Human justice must go with one or the other, but never both at the same time. Divine justice, on the other hand, manages to do both at the same time. So how does God do it? The answer consists in what we call the substitutionary atonement of Christ. Just as Adam representated us in the Garden of Eden and whose sin was imputed to us, so too did Christ represent us in his death upon the cross and whose righteousness is imputed to us. In this case not only did we get his righteousness but he got our sin. So our sin was punished in Christ who was able to bear it by the power of his divine nature. So here we see mercy and justice reconciled.
