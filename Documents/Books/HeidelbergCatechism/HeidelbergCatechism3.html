Lord's Day 5: Questions 12-15

Question 12: Since, according to God’s righteous judgment we deserve temporal and eternal punishment, how can we escape this punishment and be again received into favour?
Answer: God demands that his justice be satisfied(1). Therefore we must make full payment, either by ourselves or through another(2)

1. Ex 20:5; 23:7; Rom 2:1-11.
2. Is 53:11; Rom 8:3, 4.

Question 13: Can we by ourselves make this payment?
Answer: Certainly not. On the contrary, we daily increase our debt(1)

1. Ps 130:3; Mt 6:12; Rom 2:4, 5.

Question 12 is the most critical question any fallen human being can ask and it obsessed that great reformer, Martin Luther, prior to the Reformation. There was nothing Luther could do to pacify his conscience; from self-flagellation to making his bed in the snow, as well as hours in the confessional only convinced him that he was a doomed sinner and on his way to hell. But we notice in the Catechism's answer that there is provision for the debt of sin to be paid by another, i.e., one who could bear the weight of eternal condemnation. This substitute must himself be innocent of all sin lest he suffer eternal condemnation for his own sin and not those of others.

Question 13 asks whether we ourselves can make this payment but we already know from question 12 that the punishment is not only temporal but also eternal, which means that we would never reach the point of divine satisfaction. In fact, not only are we incapable of paying an infinite penalty but we only increase our debt for as long as we exist which is eternal.

Question 14: Can any mere creature pay for us?
Answer: No. In the first place, God will not punish another creature for the sin which man has committed.(1) Furthermore, no mere creature can sustain the burden of God’s eternal wrath against sin and deliver others from it(2).

1. Ezek 18:4, 20; Heb 2:14-18.
2. Ps 130:3; Nahum 1:6.

Question 15: What kind of mediator and deliverer must we seek?
Answer: One who is a true(1) and righteous(2) man, and yet more powerful than all creatures; that is, one who is at the same time true God(3).

1. Cor 15:21; Heb 2:17.
2. Is 53:9; 2 Cor 5:21; Heb 7:26.
3 Is 7:14; 9:6; Jer 23:6; Jn 1:1; Rom 8:3, 4.

Lord's Day 6: Questions 16-19

Question 16: Why must he be a true and righteous man?
Answer: He must be a true man because the justice of God requires that the same human nature which has sinned should pay for sin.(1) He must be a righteous man because one who himself is a sinner cannot pay for others.(2)

1. Rom 5:12, 15; 1 Cor 15:21; Heb 2:14-16.
2. Heb 7:26, 27; 1 Pet 3:18.

So let's consider the qualifications of the one who would qualify as a suitable substitute. He needs to be:
1. Human. This is necessary in order to represent human beings. Since the law of God is for human obedience, only a human being can obey it.
2. Divine. A mere human being would be crushed under the weight of eternal divine wrath. So our substitute needs to be able to hold up beneath that wrath.
3. Righteous and holy. Any moral imperfection would make him liable for eternal punishment and therefore unable to deliver others from sin.

Question 17: Why must he, at the same time, be true God?
Answer: He must be true God so that by the power of his divine nature(1) he might bear in his human nature the burden of God’s wrath(2) and might obtain for us and restore to us righteousness and life.(3)

1. Is 9:6.
2. Deut 4:24; Nahum 1:6; Ps 130:3.
3. Is 53:5, 11; Jn 3:16; 2 Cor 5:21.

The incarnation of the son of God resulted in what is called the hypostatic union which is the union of both the human and the divine natures of Christ in one person. The human nature did not include an independent human person which would have made him two persons instead of one. So Christ was not a human person but he certainly was a divine person, i.e., the eternal Son of God. This doctrine, called the Anhypostasis, was defined in 451 AD by the Council of Chalcedon, which was the fourth ecumenical council of the Christian Church.

So because Christ is not a human person he did not inherit original sin from Adam. Neither did he commit personal sins, being perfectly divine, and was therefore eminently qualified to represent us as the new Adam (Romans 5:12-21).

Question 18: But who is that Mediator who at the same time is true God and a true and righteous man?
Answer: Our Lord Jesus Christ(1) who has become for us wisdom from God – that is, our righteousness, holiness and redemption (1 Cor 1:30).

1. Mt 1:21-23; Lk 2:11; 1 Tim 2:5; 3:16.

Question 19: From where do you know this?
Answer: From the holy gospel, which God himself first revealed in Paradise(1). Later, he had it proclaimed by the patriarchs(2) and prophets(3) and foreshadowed by the sacrifices and other ceremonies of the law(4). Finally, he had it fulfilled through his only Son(5).

1. Gen 3:15.
2. Gen 12:3; 22:18; 49:10.
3. Is 53; Jer 23:5, 6; Mic 7:18-20; Acts 10:43; Heb 1:1.
4. Lev 1-7; Jn 5:46; Heb 10:1-10.
5. Rom 10:4; Gal 4:4, 5; Col 2:17.

The short answer to question 19 is that the previous answers are contained in the scriptures exclusively and are progressively revealed from Genesis 3:15 through to the books of the New Testament. In this progress we see a steady transition from shadow to substance starting with the conflict between the serpent (Satan) and his "seed" (unbelievers) on the one hand, and the seed of the woman (Christ) on the other. The Old Testament saints looked forward to the coming of the Messiah (the Christ) whereas the New Testament saints look back at that same coming. Although the time frames are different the object of faith is the same, Jesus Christ our Lord.
